Risk is calculated per event, not as a running score on the share itself, based on the event's type and its payload.

  </Card>

  <Card title="Low" icon="shield-check">
    `disableShare` — turning a share off reduces risk by definition.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Medium" icon="triangle-exclamation">
    `regenerateShare`, or a `configureShare` that removes password protection or email domain restriction without also removing enterprise-member restriction.
  </Card>

  <Card title="High" icon="fire">
    A `configureShare` that removes enterprise-member restriction, sets no email domain restriction, or removes a password that was previously set.
  </Card>

## The actual rule, condensed from the script

For a `configureShare` event:

1. If the change sets `restrictedToEnterpriseMembers` to `false`, or clears the email domain allow-list entirely → **High**.
2. If the change removes password protection _and_ the share previously had one on record → **High**.
3. If the change removes password protection generally, or disables domain restriction → **Medium**.
4. If the change enables link copying or extension visibility → **Medium**.
5. Otherwise → **Low**.

`regenerateShare` is always **Medium** (a new share ID means the old link stops working, which is disruptive but not itself a security exposure). `disableShare` is always **Low**. `enableShare` is always **Info**.

## Why this shape, specifically

The rule prioritizes the change that actually widens exposure, removing a password that existed, or dropping the enterprise-only restriction, over changes that are procedural (rotating an ID) or restrictive (turning a share off). A share getting _more_ locked down should never rank above one that just lost its only protection, and the ordering in the script reflects that directly: the checks for restriction and password removal run first and return early, before anything softer gets evaluated.
